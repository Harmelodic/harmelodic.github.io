# Vinyl Players and Hi-Fi Systems

Vinyl players, also called turntables, record players, gramophones, or phonographs are devices that play vinyl records.

"Hi-Fi systems" is an umbrella term to refer to the various devices that, when used together, can produce records
(things like _amplifiers_ or _amps_, and _speakers_).

In 2017, I bought a shit vinyl player and was very disappointed with it. I resolved to not do that again and instead do
a bunch of research into what made a good vinyl player, and then find an affordable one that met those requirements. I
then wanted to pair that with a decent Hi-Fi system that could be used to listen to the records as well as be used as
the speaker system whenever I wanted to listen to music on Spotify, or when watching films / TV shows.

Since doing all that, several people have asked my opinion on what makes a good vinyl player & Hi-Fi system. So... why
not write that up for anyone / everyone? In fact, you're probably reading this, because you asked me and I pointed you
to here!

Now, you could go all audiophile here and buy some very expensive stuff, but that's not the goal. The goal is listen to
vinyl records / audio using quality equipment to ensure that the audio is _good_, the equipment will last a long time,
the vinyl player won't damage your records when playing them, and it won't cost you thousands of pounds. Be warned
though, that this stuff is a luxury and so won't be cheap. Treat it like an investment in having good audio in your home
for the rest of your life.

## Vinyl Players

First off: Don't just go to a second hand store or Amazon or some other place and buy a cheap or cool-looking vinyl
player - not only will it sound terrible, but it will likely damage your records over time and make them sound worse and
worse.

Quick overview of the anatomy of a vinyl player:

- **Base** - the main body of the vinyl player.
- **Deck** - the spinning bit where you place the records for playing.
- **Arm** - (aka tonearm) the long bit that extends over the deck, with the "needle" on the end, and with weights on the
  other end.
- **Needle** - (aka stylus) the delicate bit of metal on the end of the arm that touches the record, often part of a
  "cartridge" piece to allow easy replacing of worn/broken needles without it being too fiddly.
- **Lid** - the bit that covers the whole body to protect the vinyl player and record from dust (etc.) whilst playing.
- **Speed switcher** - a switch or part of the player that allows you to switch how fast the deck spins.
- **Power switch** - Turns the vinyl player on and off, on some vinyl players this might be the same switch as the speed
  switcher, or might just be a power switch that then starts the vinyl player spinning.
- **Audio output** port or cable (e.g. Phono) - the place on vinyl player where the audio signal is output.

The signs of a good vinyl player as follows:

- When stood, it stands flat, and you can adjust the feet to ensure flatness - this prevents the needle from slipping
  and possibly damaging the record.
- Can play at different speeds (usually 33 ⅓ and 45 rpm, and maybe 78 rpm) for playing LPs and single records.
- Has weights on the arm to adjust for perfect needle contact to ensure quality audio reproduction and minimising damage
  to the record - you can get neat little bits of card and plastic measurers to help you perfectly balance the arm to
  ensure the needle is resting too heavily on the record (and doing damage), nor too lightly (leading to slippage) and
  has a slight bit of tension/weight that pulls the arm inwards, to ensure the needle doesn't slip out of the record
  groove.
- A lid to prevent dust collecting on the record and other accidents from resulting in stuff breaking an open/exposed
  vinyl player.
- Replaceable needles / cartridges for when needles wear out and so you can repair your vinyl player rather than need to
  toss it out when the needle wears out.
- Phono out - many vinyl players try to do too much as an "all in one" solution, but then cheapen out on lots of parts
  to make it affordable. Skip all of this by just getting a vinyl player that outputs the raw quiet phono signal, that
  needs to be put through an amplifier (amp) before being output to speakers. This was the case in 2017, but many modern
  vinyl players may now output an audio signal via USB or that has gone through a pre-amp and is still affordable.
- If using phono: A ground wire to prevent hum in the audio signal.

When researching for vinyl players with these features, you bump into a lot of brands and recommendations by audiophiles
to get specific expensive equipment. Whilst a lot of it is expensive, I eventually found the company _Pro-Ject Audio
Systems_ which produces a range of vinyl players with a minimalist look, all the features I wanted, at a reasonable
price.

I was lucky enough to find a second-hand _Pro-Ject Essential Phono_ being sold on a local marketplace for about £80 or
£100 (2017 on Gumtree).

Note: Buy vinyl records that are in good shape! If you buy scratched and beaten up vinyl records, you'll get a crap
sound - so double-check the vinyl record for scratches and scuffs before buying it.

## Hi-Fi System

Fundamentally, a Hi-Fi system is made up of:

- Any number of input devices, like vinyl players, DVD players, games consoles, TVs, and radios.
- An amplifier that can receive these input signals to amplify and adjust the audio quality signal and route the signal
  to some outputs.
- Speakers to listen to the audio.
- Speaker cables to connect the amp to the speakers.

You can get extra devices like equalizers to further adjust the audio signal before or after the amplification, but it's
not necessary and decent quality input devices, amplifiers, speaker cables and speakers will produce a very nice sound.

Amplifier needs to have:

- Enough power (wattage) to drive the speakers
- Enough inputs as needed (e.g. phono for vinyl player, multiple lines in for devices like TVs, Chromecast Audios, CD
  players, etc.)
- Enough outputs for the number of speakers you want (recommend just going stereo, as surround-sound is a bit overrated
  unless you have a lot of money and really like watching films).
- Basic Bass & Treble controls.
- Master volume control.

Speakers need to have:

- 2x speakers for stereo sound
- Doesn't blow out speakers at high volume
- Has a relatively flat audio profile (decent and even lows, mids and highs) at all volume levels.
- Good size for the space required
- Durable build quality.
- A cover for the actual speakers to protect them against dust/debris.

I ended up buying a _Yamaha A-S201_ amplifier, a pair of _Cambridge Audio SX-60_ speakers, and 2x 1 metre-long speaker
cables from _Richer Sounds_ in Manchester for £308 (2017 pricing).
