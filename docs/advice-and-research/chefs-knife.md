# Chef's Knife

Lots of different knives for use in the kitchen, but a _chef's knife_ (aka a cook's knife) is a medium to large knife
for general use when preparing food. A good one makes it easier.

A chef's knife is different from a "santoku" knife, though their size and use are very similar. The santoku knife has
a "sheep's foot" tip (and usually lacks a full bolster).

Take the following aspects into account when looking to get a chef's knife and see what you like:

## Knife anatomy

It's useful to know the terms:

- Point: The pointy bit at the end, and should be pointy for piercing things.
- Tip: The sharp bit on the edge of the knife near the point. Should be sharp and thin for delicate slicing work.
- Belly: The main flat body of the knife.
- Edge: The main sharp bit of the knife used for chopping things.
- Spine: The blunt back of the knife that faces up.
- Heel: The bit of the belly of the knife that is closest to the handle.
- Bolster: The bit where the blade joins the handle.
- Tang: The continuation of the blade metal that goes into and runs through the handle.
- Handle: The part you hold / grip.
- Butt: The blunt end of the knife.

## Blade material

The blade should be durable and thus made from a material you're happy to use and keep sharp:

- Stainless steel is pretty classic, relatively inexpensive and will need regularly sharpening to keep sharp.

- Carbon steel contains more carbon in the steel composition, which makes it harder and so less prone to deformation,
  but comes with a bunch of downsides: More brittle (so more likely to chip or break), doesn't hold an edge for as long
  so needs more regular sharpening, and it's also more expensive.

- Damascus steel is a kind of high-carbon steel that is specially forged using the wootz process to form a very strong
  and durable steel, with pretty swirls on the blade that are a byproduct of the forging process. The result is a
  strong, shatter-resistant blade, that holds an edge for a long time and so needs less maintenance.

- Ceramic knifes are made of... ceramic materials rather than steel. What results is a blade that is very sharp and also
  holds its edge for a very long time but are very brittle and so chip easily. This brittleness also means that it's
  dangerous/difficult to sharpen the knives at home and so it's recommended to take them to a professional to get the
  resharpened (when they eventually do need resharpening).

Personally, I like stainless steel or damascus steel. The others aren't worth the trade off.

## Blade manufacturing

Knives are either forged or stamped.

Forged are made in forges and usually result in more durable, sharper, more ergonomic, easier to use knives. This also
makes them more expensive.

Stamped knives are literally stamped out of big sheets of metal, and then sharpened as needed. They are typically less
durable and generally poorer in quality. They are super cheap though, because they can be made easily/quickly.

I recommend going for a forged knife.

## Blade length

A chef's knife is usually about 20 cm long (7.87"). This strikes a useful balance between being big enough to handle
large chopping and slicing tasks (butchering or chopping large vegetables) but being not so big that it's too heavy or
difficult to wield.

## Granton edges

Some knives have "Granton edges", which are small indentations on the belly of the blade near the edge. As well as
looking interesting, they are supposed to make it easier to slice things because there is less contact between the belly
of the blade and the food. It can also reportedly help food sticking less to the blade during chopping.

I'm sceptical at their functional benefit, and they typically increase the price of the knife more than comfortable.

## Bolster

A bolster (aka a "finger guard" or "shoulder") the bit of the blade that connects to the handle. The type of bolster
used affects safety, weight/feel and the ability to sharpen the knife.

You can either have _full_ bolster or a _half_ bolster.

A full bolster is a thick blunt bit of metal that blocks the heel of the knife from the user's fingers. This provides
increased finger protection from the knife (in case of slippage), but can make the blade heavier and can make it harder
to full sharpen the edge of the blade.

A half bolster is when there is no guard in place, but simply the thick bit of the blade that connects to handle. This
reduces the safety feature of the bolster, but makes the blade lighter and makes it easier to fully sharpen the edge of
the knife.

Unless you're very comfortable with a knife (i.e. you're a chef), I recommend a full bolster.

## Handle material

Handle material affects feel (weight and shape), looks but primarily: washing.

- **Wood** is a sustainable material but means you can't wash the knife if the dishwasher. It also requires regular
  maintenance (cleaning and oiling). If left unclean or untreated, wood can become grimy and slippery and even allow for
  bacterial or fungal growth. Since wood is wood, it is easily repairable/replaceable though.

- **Stabilised wood** is basically wood that has been covered in resin, so fills up the gaps in the wood (where water
  and grime might get) with resin. This makes the wood more durable, less prone to getting grimy or mouldy, and so needs
  less maintenance. Stabilised wood can be dishwasher-safe, but it's good to check the manufacturer's guidelines.
  Obviously, the resin makes it less of a [sustainable material](./sustainable-materials.md) though. This also makes the
  wood a unique look, which some find quite pretty (personally, I don't).

- **Plastic** is pretty common. It's cheap, durable, easy to clean, doesn't require treatment and many plastic knives
  can be dishwasher safe (some are not though). Sustainability-wise, it leaves much to be desired.

- **Composite** (e.g. Micarta or G-10) are other materials usually made of fibrous materials that covered in resin
  (like stabilised wood). This creates a durable, easy-to-clean and unique looking handle (I'm not fan, personally).
  Similar to stabilised wood, it's not the most sustainable choice.

- **Metal** can also be used, often as just a continuation of the blade metal, with small indentations where rubber is
  glued to improve grip. This is a durable solution, but I found makes it harder to clean and can be slippery /
  dangerous to use.

- **Other** materials like **bone**, **leather** wrappings or **ivory** can be used, which can be more
  environmentally-friendly options and provide a unique look, though can be expensive and come with questionable ethics.

If you are OK with the maintenance, then I recommend wood. Otherwise, just go for hard plastic as all other options are
pure vanity and are needlessly expensive.

## Feel

Feel is about how the knife feels in your hand which affects ease of use and ultimately affects your confidence in using
the knife. If you're not confident, you're more prone to making mistakes like cutting yourself or chopping/slicing your
food in an undesired way.

Handle shape can be round, angular or more ergonomically designed - which affects how the knife looks but also how it
feels. I recommend an ergonomic or round design for best feel.

The weight of the knife and how that weight is distributed also affects feel. Too heavy and it can feel like effort to
lift and use the knife, but too light and the more force and effort you need to put in (where the weight of the knife
can help with slicing and chopping). If the blade is too heavy, it can make the knife harder to use and be delicate
with, but if the weight is all in the handle, then the weight of the blade won't be helping with chopping, and it can be
a sign of lack of durability in the blade.
