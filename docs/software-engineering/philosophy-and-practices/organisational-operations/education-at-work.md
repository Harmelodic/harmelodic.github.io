# Education at work

Learning does not stop after completing a Computer Science degree.

Everyone is constantly learning new things all the time. A lot of people learn in different ways, but I've observed that
a lot of engineers people learn by doing, and learn by being taught (often by senior engineers or tech leads, etc.).

As someone who enjoys teaching and software engineering, I've found it odd and often frustrating how little education we
provide to software engineers after regular higher education (e.g. a Computer Science degree) and nowadays, there are
plenty of software engineers working in the industry without a degree in a software engineering-aligned field.

## The current state

What tends to happen in the workplace is that we focus on the following:

- Self-improvement / Self-learning - which puts a lot of onus on individuals to upskill themselves when they might lack
  the time, skill or circumstances to make that possible, and their career and productivity suffers.
- Feedback - which is often done poorly (creating deeper insecurities) and even when done well usually focuses more on
  self-improvement.
- Learn by exposure - in that people will learn how to do things simply by being exposed to them. This often suffices in
  order for people to be able to produce code that works, but they often lacks the broad or deeper knowledge or
  big-picture perspective needed to produce high-quality software and quickly and in a principled but practical manner.
- Knowledge-sharing between teams - which usually fails due to over-siloing of teams, but also fails when lacking good
  technical leadership and management to effectively facilitate such knowledge sharing.

We can, and should do better.

## A few reality checks

First, I think some realities need to be checked, with some clear statements:

1. Not everyone is a self-starter, and that's OK.
2. Not everyone will read architecture / patterns books, or management and leadership books / articles, or the latest
   Martin Fowler posts, or what the latest trendiest methodology is, and that's OK.
3. A person does not need to have a Computer Science degree (or equivalent) in order to become a software engineer, but
   it certainly helps.
4. Not everyone learns by doing, and even those who do can often plateau how much they learn, since "things work".
5. If we want to see people learn and improve, we must provide them the support and tools needed for them to achieve
   that.

## Learning from existing models

We must get better at engineering education, _in the workplace_.

This reminds me of models like apprenticeships
and [teaching hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching_hospital) - where not
only is work carried out, but education is given to future and current professionals in a real setting where they are
supervised and supported.

As Wikipedia puts describes teaching hospitals, when speaking of these educational residency programs:

> The purpose of these residency programs is to create an environment where new doctors can learn to practice medicine
> in a safe setting which is supervised by physicians that provide both oversight and education.

Whilst we're not saving lives and curing illnesses and have the possibility of letting people die, I think there's
something to learn from this way of approaching education. For doctors, they don't end their education and get an MD and
then go into work and start treating people, they go a place where they will treat people under supervision and learn
more until they are deemed experienced enough to do so without supervision.

Imagine if an organisation (or every organisation) making software did that for their engineers:

- Junior software engineers could become more productive and skilled faster.
- Regular software engineers could become more experienced and knowledgeable faster and need less leadership and
  supervision.
- Senior software engineers could spend less time running around trying to keep everybody aligned and instead delegate
  and trust more, since everyone is more knowledgeable and aligned, and also teach more, to keep the ecosystem alive.

## Making it happen

Personally, I think there are two blockers to this happening in many organisations:

- Education is a lower priority and ends up almost never being prioritised.
- Senior engineers / technical leadership have egos that enjoy being experience & knowledgeable and fear sharing that
  experience & knowledge because then others could do their job, and they'd be less important.

The former is a choice, and one I simply must ask people to reconsider. The latter is achievable by either not hiring
these overly-egotistical people or making it part of their job responsibility to educate and share knowledge.

Unblocking these two things can spark some initial education where learning takes place. More workshops, sessions and
discussions later, and you'll have a growing learning culture.

The goal is to have respectful and continuous education be part of the normal operations of the organisation.
