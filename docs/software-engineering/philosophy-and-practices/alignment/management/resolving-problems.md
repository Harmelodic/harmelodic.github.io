# Resolving Problems

A couple of management's functions are to "resolve systemic problems" and "resolving conflicts between workers".

I've observed different levels of competency at performing these functions and found that a common feature of poor
management is to not actually resolve the problem.

Here is some guidance and not only resolving the problem, but resolving it in a way that improves the worker-manager
relationship.

When workers speak out about problems, managers must _listen_ and _act appropriately_.

## Listening

Workers want to feel listened to and that their concerns are being considered, even if their concerns are invalid,
unhelpful, rude or intolerant.

- If workers feel listened to, then this improves the worker-manager relationship, and makes workers more receptive to
  feedback (positive and negative) from the manager.
- If workers does not feel listen to, they tend to feel ignored and debased, and it can seed or grow resentment for
  management - thus breaking down trust and the worker-manager relationship.
- If workers feel like they are being silenced, this tends to quickly drive up resentment and frustration, and further
  break down trust and the worker-manager relationship.

## Acting appropriately

If a manager was to not act appropriately, the manager is shirking their responsibility of resolving problems. This can
actually be worse in the long-term than not listening, as the worker will later feel betrayed by the manager (because
the problem was not solved), which can seed deeper resentment and frustration with management.

In order to determine how to act, a manager must seek an answer the following question:

> Is the problem raised a legitimate problem?

I recommend not asking this directly to workers, as they tend to respond defensively and they feel ignored / not
listened to.

### Legitimate problems

For problems that are known to be legitimate, there are a few options to the manager:

1. Listen, and then address the problem for the worker.
2. Listen, and team up with the worker to address the problem.
3. Listen, and empower the worker to solve the problem for themselves.
4. Listen, but do nothing
5. Don't listen, and do nothing
6. Attempt to silence the worker.

As covered above in [listening](#listening) and [acting appropriately](#acting-appropriately), options #4, #5 and #6 are
quite unacceptable.

Options #1, #2 and #3 are therefore the only acceptable paths forward, and should be adopted. The choice of these
options is usually dependent on the problem raised, the ability of the worker, and whether the manager has the time and
readiness to solve the problem for the worker.

### Unknown problems

Some problems are difficult to determine whether they are legitimate or not. In these cases, there are a few options to
the manager:

1. Listen, and inform the worker they'll get back to them - then actually do.
2. Listen, but do nothing
3. Don't listen, and do nothing
4. Attempt to silence the worker.

As covered above in [listening](#listening) and [acting appropriately](#acting-appropriately), options #2, #3 and #4 are
quite unacceptable.

Option #1 provides an opportunity to buy time for the manager to discover the legitimacy of the problem. When it is
confirmed to be legitimate or not, the manager can return to the worker and act appropriately. If the legitimacy of the
problem continues to be in question, then the manager should opt for transparency and be honest with the worker and
inform them of the reason for the delay, but they are continuing to investigate to ensure that the problem (if it
exists) will be resolved.

### Illegitimate problems

Some problems are illegitimate, and the worker needs to be informed of this. Once again, the manager has a few options:

1. Listen, and help the worker unpack their problem / perspective.
2. Listen, and provide information to the worker.
3. Listen, but do nothing
4. Don't listen, and do nothing
5. Attempt to silence the worker / tell them off / outwardly judge the worker.

As covered above in [listening](#listening) and [acting appropriately](#acting-appropriately), options #3, #4 and #5 are
quite unacceptable. I've included "tell them off" and "outwardly judge the worker" to Option #4, as to the worker, they
are often indistinguishable.

Option #1 is for situations where the worker has a problematic perspective and provides an opportunity for the worker to
unpack their perspective. As the worker does this, it is helpful to ask nudging questions to prompt and guide the worker
to reflect and correct their perspective themselves. If done with compassion, and thus without outward judgement or
"telling off", this prevents the worker from feeling defensive which further aids with providing feedback, and it can
even prompt thanks from the worker at helping them realise they were in the wrong.

Of course, workers may ultimately simply take a different view to the manager or the organisation. In these cases, the
manager should encourage the worker to reflect on their perspective, and reflect themselves on their own perspective
(even discuss with other managers or workers to aid this reflection).
After a short period of reflection, the worker and manager should reconvene, and if conflict between perspectives still
exists, the manager should inform the worker that their perspective is not currently accepted, and they must accept
this (sometimes referred to as "disagree and commit").

Option #2 is for situations where the worker has raised an illegitimate problem based on lack of information. In this
case the manager is responsible for informing the worker of this information and helping them understand that their
problem is not actually a problem (or an unfortunately unsolvable problem).  
It is a good idea, if choosing this option for the manager to reflect on why the worker didn't have this information in
the first place and if there should be some efforts made to improve transparency. Informing workers of this reflection
and that they will attempt to be more transparent can further build trust and the worker-manager relationship.

## Preventing problems

In firefighting, it is better to prevent a fire from happening than fighting one that's already started.

In disease prevention, it is better to prevent the spread of diseases, than it is to cure them.

In management, it is better to prevent problems from happening, than it is to resolve them. Ironically, management are
often the _cause_ of problems, through practices like "micromanagement".

To prevent problems, managers should focus more on improving things workers [value](../values.md), especially
transparency, inclusivity and trust, and offloading hindering organisational administrative work from workers. Provided
these are functioning well, managers should work with workers to experiment with different ways of working to improve
organisational [alignment](../index.md), worker happiness, and productivity.

## Final remarks

As you may have noticed, there is a lot of expectation and responsibility on a manager to act appropriately when
problems are raised. Management can be a thankless job, and there are lots of opportunities for management to lose the
trust and respect of the workers they manage.

This does not make any of the above less important, but it is also important for workers to recognise this and provide
empathy, consideration, and thanks to managers for their good work and efforts.
